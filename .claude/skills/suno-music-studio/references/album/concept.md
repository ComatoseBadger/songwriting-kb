# Album & EP Concept Development

How to turn an idea into a planned album or EP: format, type, the planning phases, tracklist architecture, pacing, motifs, and, for true-story records, the research method that has to come before any lyrics.

If the user has stated preferences (track counts, intro/outro rules, themes to explore or avoid, duration targets, source standards), they win over the defaults here. Preferences shape the plan without limiting the creative work.

---

## Core Principles

- **Every album has an arc.** Even with non-narrative tracks, plan an emotional journey, a thematic exploration, a sonic progression, and how the listener moves through it.
- **Sequencing is everything.** Track order makes or breaks the record: momentum, emotional flow, peaks and valleys, an opening statement and a closing resolution.
- **Constraints breed creativity.** Limits on genre, theme, or format force interesting choices. Use them.
- **The concept serves the music.** A clever concept with weak songs fails. The music has to stand on its own without liner notes.
- **When in doubt, cut.** A tight 8-track album beats a bloated 15-track slog.

## Single, EP, Album, or Double?

Ask first: *why does this need to be an album rather than single tracks?* If the idea is one strong hook or one moment, it is a single. If it is one idea explored from a few angles, it is an EP. If it needs room for an arc with peaks, valleys, and a payoff, it is an album.

| Format | Tracks | Best for | Shape |
|--------|--------|----------|-------|
| **Single** | 1-2 | One hook, one moment, testing a sound, a lead-in to a larger release | Strongest version of one idea; optional B-side or alternate version |
| **EP** | 4-6 | Testing a new direction, one concept explored deeply, introducing a new artist, tightly focused themes | Intro → Development → Climax → Resolution |
| **Standard album** | 8-12 | Complete statements, narrative albums, established artist projects | Three acts or thematic sections |
| **Double album** | 15+ | Epic stories needing space, comprehensive documentaries, sprawling concepts | Movements, sections, or discs with distinct identities. Every track has to justify the length. |

Default track duration: 3:30-5:00. Use 2:00-3:00 for punk and other fast genres, and 5:00-7:00 for prog and post-rock.

## Album Types

| Type | Definition | Key questions |
|------|------------|---------------|
| **Documentary** | Real events, factual storytelling | Timeline, sources, angle |
| **Narrative** | Fictional or dramatized story across tracks | Protagonist, conflict, arc |
| **Thematic** | United by a theme, mood, or idea, not plot | Sub-themes, emotional journey |
| **Character Study** | Deep dive into one person, real or fictional | Facets, time periods, through-line |
| **Collection** | Standalone songs with a loose connection | Unifying element, flow |
| **OST** | Music for a fictional media property's world and moments | Media type, world, leitmotifs, vocal/instrumental mix |

### Choosing between similar types

- **Documentary vs Character Study:** focus on *events and timeline* → Documentary. Focus on *a person's inner life, growth, and contradictions* → Character Study. A hacker's arrest → Documentary. What made them who they are → Character Study.
- **Character Study vs Thematic:** is the person the *subject* or a *lens* for broader themes? An album about Snowden's choices → Character Study. An album about surveillance that uses Snowden as one example → Thematic.
- **Documentary vs Narrative:** real and sourced → Documentary, which brings research, verification, and the narrator-voice constraint. Fictional → Narrative, which leaves full creative freedom.
- **OST vs Narrative:** follows a plot with characters → Narrative. Builds a fictional property's functional soundscape (levels, scenes, episodes) → OST.
- **OST vs Thematic:** abstract theme ("digital isolation") → Thematic. Concrete fictional world with locations and story moments (a cyberpunk RPG, a noir detective film) → OST.
- **Still unclear:** ask the user, "Is this album more about the events, the person, or the theme?"

### Planning by type

**Documentary / true crime**
- Questions: What's the central event? Who are the key players? What's the timeline? What sources exist? What's the angle, meaning why it matters and not only what happened? How many tracks does the story need?
- Structures: *chronological* (first track = beginning, last = resolution); *thematic* (group by theme, not time); *character-focused* (each track follows a different person); *event-focused* (each track = a critical moment).
- Example: the Great Molasses Flood (Boston, 1919). Tracks 1-4 "The Monster" (the neighborhood, the tank, ignored warnings); 5-8 "The Wave" (the disaster, chaos, death, heroism); 9-11 "The Reckoning" (the lies, the trial, the verdict).
- See the true-story section below for the research method.

**Narrative**
- Questions: Who is the protagonist? What do they want? What stands in the way? How does it resolve? What's the emotional arc?
- Structures: *three-act* (setup 1-3, conflict 4-8, resolution 9-12); *character journey* (each track = a stage of development); *multiple perspectives* (different characters tell the same story); *non-linear* (fragments that connect by the end).
- Example (addiction recovery): 1-3 Descent, 4-6 Recognition, 7-9 Struggle, 10-12 Emergence.

**Thematic**
- Questions: What's the central theme? What are its facets? What emotion does each track evoke? How do tracks connect without plot? What's the sonic throughline?
- Structures: *exploration* (each track takes a different angle); *emotional arc* (a journey through related feelings); *contrasts* (light/dark, hope/despair, past/future); *mosaic* (pieces that form a larger picture).
- Example ("Surrender"): tracks explore giving up, letting go, acceptance, trust, defeat, and peace, moving from resistance to release.

**Character Study**
- Questions: Who is this person? Which aspects will you explore? Which time periods? What's the through-line? What makes them compelling?
- Structures: *life stages* (childhood → youth → adulthood → legacy); *facets* (father, CEO, addict, visionary); *defining moments*; *internal/external* (alternate inner thoughts and outer actions).
- Example (Edward Snowden): childhood, NSA career, discovery of the surveillance programs, the decision to leak, the Hong Kong escape, exile, legacy. A real person is also a true-story album, so it follows the research rules.

**Collection**
- Questions: What's the unifying element, however loose? Genre consistency or variety? Intentional flow, or is variety the point?
- Structures: *best foot forward* (strongest first); *energy arc*; *palate cleanser* (vary styles to prevent fatigue); *bookends* (strong opener and closer, looser middle).
- Example: one song per Linux distro, ordered by release date, which creates an accidental history of Linux.

**OST (original soundtrack)**
- Questions: Which media type (game, film, TV, anime, theater, podcast)? What world or setting? Which scenes map to tracks (levels, chases, boss fights, quiet moments, title, credits)? Which recurring leitmotifs? What mix of vocal and instrumental? What genre palette? What audience journey (discovery, escalation, climax, resolution)?
- Structures: *scene-based*, *act-based*, *world-based* (each track a location with its own genre and mood), *episode-based* (escalating stakes), *gameplay loop* (title → explore → combat → boss → victory → rest).
- Duration by scene function:

| Scene function | Target | Section strategy |
|----------------|--------|------------------|
| Title / opening | ~1:30 | `[Intro]` → `[Main Theme]` → `[End]` |
| Quiet moment / menu | ~2:00-3:00 | `[Intro]` → `[Theme]` → `[Variation]` → `[Outro]` |
| Exploration / dialogue | ~3:00-4:00 | Standard structure, 2-3 sections |
| Action / boss fight | ~4:00-5:00+ | Several builds and drops, "extended" in the style prompt |
| Cutscene / flashback | ~1:30-2:30 | `[Intro]` → `[Theme]` → `[Outro]` → `[End]` |
| Credits / ending | ~3:00-4:00 | Reprise sections with theme callbacks |

Fewer section tags give shorter tracks, and `[End]` is the strongest stop signal. Expect 2-3 generations to hit a target length, and trim in post.

- Example (retro sci-fi platformer): 01 Title Screen (~1:30, chiptune) → 02-04 Early Levels (~3:00, upbeat electronic, rising tempo) → 05 First Boss (~4:30, synth-metal) → 06 Hub World (~2:30, ambient respite) → 07-09 Late Levels (~3:30, darker orchestral-electronic) → 10 Final Boss (~5:00, full orchestral + electronic) → 11 Credits (~3:30, piano reprise of the title theme).
- Example (noir detective film): 01 Opening Titles (smoky jazz sax) → 02 The Case (brushed drums, muted trumpet) → 03 Late Night Stakeout (rain, piano) → 04 The Chase (uptempo jazz, brass stabs) → 05 Betrayal (slow strings, dissonant piano) → 06 Showdown (orchestra + jazz ensemble) → 07 Closing Credits (bittersweet reprise).

Mixed albums, OSTs especially, often combine vocal tracks for key story moments with instrumental tracks for atmosphere and transitions. Mark each track's vocal or instrumental status during structure planning. Instrumental tracks skip lyric writing and go straight to the style prompt (see references/suno/prompting.md).

## The 7 Planning Phases

Get explicit answers to all seven phases before writing any track. **Batch each phase's questions into one message** as a numbered list with brief context for each, and let the user answer them together. Asking questions one at a time turns planning into 30+ turns. After each phase, summarize what was decided, then move on.

**Phase 1: Foundation**
1. Artist: existing or new?
2. Genre: which sonic palette? (Check references/genres/<genre-slug>/README.md.)
3. Type: Documentary, Narrative, Thematic, Character Study, Collection, or OST?
4. Scale: single, EP (4-6), standard (8-12), or double (15+)?
5. Central theme, story, event, or character?
6. True story? If yes, the research requirements below apply.

**Phase 2: Concept deep dive**
- Every type: What's the central story, theme, or message? Who are the key characters or subjects? What's the arc? What's the emotional core? Why this story, for the artist and for the audience?
- Documentary: key events, available sources, angle (chronological, thematic, or perspective), and the emotional truth being conveyed.
- Narrative: character, plot, emotional arc.
- Thematic: central theme, sub-themes, motifs.
- OST: media type, world, scene mapping, leitmotif strategy, genre palette, instrumental mix.

**Phase 3: Sonic direction**
- Reference artists and albums
- Production style: dark/bright, minimal/dense, organic/synthetic
- Vocal approach: narrator, character voices, sung, rapped, or mixed. If one voice should carry the whole album, plan a persona (see references/album/personas-and-covers.md).
- Instrumentation palette
- Mood and atmosphere
- Target track duration

**Phase 4: Structure planning**
- Track breakdown: How many tracks does the concept need? For each track: working title, core focus, connection to the whole, and vocal or instrumental.
- Sequencing: lay out a rough order → map energy → check that the story or theme progresses → pick the opener and closer → place the centerpiece (tracks 5-7) → adjust pacing.
- Refinement: Does every track earn its place? Is anything redundant? Are there gaps in the story? Does the opener hook? Does the closer satisfy?
- Seed the motif plan (see Thematic Coherence).

**Phase 5: Album art (concept only)**
- Imagery, color palette, mood, symbolic elements. Generate the art later. See references/album/art.md.

**Phase 6: Practical details**
- Album title and track titles final, or open to adjustment?
- Research needs identified (true-story albums)?
- Explicit content expected? (See references/lyrics/compliance.md.)
- Distributor genre categories? (See references/release/release.md.)

**Phase 7: Confirmation**
- Present the complete plan and ask: **"Ready to start writing?"**
- Start writing only after an explicit yes ("looks good", "yes, go"). If the user agrees only in part, revise the relevant phase. Don't start writing.

## Tracklist Architecture

**Opener**
- Makes an impact within 30 seconds
- Represents the album's core identity
- Is the best *introduction*, which is not necessarily the best track

**Closer**
- Delivers the emotional payoff and the thematic conclusion
- Leaves the listener satisfied but wanting more
- Often ties back to the opener

**Middle**
- Never put two slow songs in a row.
- Vary tempo and energy.
- Put the strongest tracks at **3, 7, and 10**. Front-load quality, especially tracks 1 and 3.

**The heart (tracks 5-7)**
- The most important thematic statement and the emotional centerpiece: what the album is really about.

**Title track**
- Include one when the album name *is* the core concept and one song explains it.
- Skip it when the name is abstract or no single track captures the whole concept.

## Pacing & Dynamics

### Energy map

Draw the album's energy as a curve and show it to the user for review:

```
01 (Intro):  ▂▂▂ Low, atmospheric
02:          ▅▅▅ Building
03:          ▇▇▇ Peak (first single)
04:          ▄▄▄ Mid-energy
05:          ▂▂▂ Valley (breather)
06:          ▆▆▆ Building again
07:          ████ Peak (centerpiece)
08:          ▅▅▅ Sustained
09:          ▃▃▃ Wind down
10 (Outro):  ▂▂▂ Resolution
```

Target shape: **Build → Peak → Valley → Build → Peak → Resolution.**
- Change energy every 2-3 tracks, and never hold one energy level for more than two consecutive tracks.
- Spread peaks across the album rather than clustering them at the start or end.
- Alternate tempo bands, so a fast track sits next to a mid- or slow-tempo track.
- Balance heavy and light: serious → playful → serious works as a palate cleanser.

### Pacing problems checklist

- Three or more songs in a row at the same energy
- Adjacent tracks within 10 BPM of each other (no contrast)
- All the high-energy tracks clustered together
- Emotional tone that never evolves across the album

**Fixes:** swap positions, suggest a tempo change, or name the track that needs rewriting for contrast.

## Thematic Coherence

- **Lyrical motifs:** repeated phrases, images, and metaphors
- **Sonic motifs:** recurring sounds, instruments, and melodic figures (leitmotifs)
- **Structural motifs:** parallel song structures, and a closer that mirrors the opener

During Phase 4, start a motif plan with three short tables, and update them as lyrics get written:

## Questions to Ask the Artist

- **Concept:** What are you trying to say? Why an album rather than singles? What should listeners feel?
- **Sonic:** What should it sound like? Which reference albums or artists? One consistent genre or varied?
- **Scope:** How many tracks feel right? How deep into the topic?

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Problem | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Too much setup | Tracks 1-3 are all introduction | Start in the middle and fill in backstory as needed |
| Flat arc | Every track has the same energy | Plan peaks and valleys deliberately |
| No payoff | Builds, then ends abruptly | The closer must deliver the emotional resolution |
| Burying the hit | The best track sits at #9 | Front-load quality (tracks 1 and 3) |
| Concept over music | Clever idea, dull songs | The concept serves the music |
| Too much explanation | Needs liner notes to make sense | The songs must stand on their own |

## True-Story & Documentary Albums

A true-story album (documentary, true crime, a character study of a real person, history) adds a hard rule: **research and human source verification come before any lyrics.**

**Order of work:** plan (7 phases) → research → assemble research notes and a source list → the user verifies the sources → write lyrics from verified facts only → check the lyrics against the sources → generate.

### Source tiers

| Tier | What | Use |
|------|------|-----|
| **1. Primary** | Court filings, transcripts, verdicts, sentencing memos; agency reports and enforcement filings; regulatory filings; full-length speeches and interviews; contemporaneous records; original posts with timestamps | Required for every key fact. Read in full. |
| **2. Investigative journalism** | Long-form original reporting (roughly 3,000+ words) by reporters with a track record that cites primary sources (ProPublica, Reuters Investigates, the WSJ Theranos investigation) | Acceptable, but verify key facts against the primary sources it cites and check for corrections |
| **3. Expert analysis** | Legal analysis, technical explanations, industry context, comparable cases | Context only, never for establishing facts or quotes |
| **4. Background** | Wikipedia, encyclopedias, timeline compilations | A starting point for the timeline, the cast list, and leads to primary sources. Never cite as a fact source. |

Press releases (DOJ, SEC, company announcements) work as an *index*: use them to find the underlying filing, then cite the filing.

### Research method

1. **Get primary sources first.** Read the actual document, not a news summary of it. Read the relevant sections fully, not by keyword search, and note what comes before and after each passage.
2. **Triple-verify key facts.** Dates, times, locations, financial figures, legal outcomes (charges, convictions, sentences), direct quotes, and chronological sequences each need **3+ independent sources**. If the user has set a lower bar for low-stakes background, follow it, but keep 3 sources for anything the lyrics state as fact.
3. **Cite at page level.** Write "Indictment p.47 ¶112", not "the indictment says", and "Transcript Day 23, pp.1847-1849, lines 12-28", not "trial testimony".
4. **Investigate, don't just collect.** Build an exact-date timeline, trace financial flows (who paid whom, when, how much), map relationships, compare to similar cases, and name the gaps (what's missing or undisclosed).
5. **Know the counter-evidence.** For each major claim, record the defense or opposing argument, the contradicting evidence, how the claim was challenged, and what remains unresolved.
6. **Record discrepancies openly.** When sources conflict, use the higher-tier source (court > government > journalism), record the conflict, and mention it in the lyric if it matters.

**Verification matrix (per key fact):**
```
FACT: [statement]
SOURCE 1: [citation] - PRIMARY/SECONDARY
SOURCE 2: [citation] - PRIMARY/SECONDARY
SOURCE 3: [citation] - PRIMARY/SECONDARY
VERIFIED: ✅/⚠️/❌ [X sources, Y primary, alignment]
```

**Evidence chain:**
```
1. [Claim] (Date) - Source: [Name](URL), p.X → [key fact]
2. [Connected claim] (Date) - Source: [Name](URL) → [key fact]
3. [Discrepancy]: $X unaccounted → Source: [Name](URL)
```

**Discrepancy, counter-evidence, and unresolved formats:**
```
DISCREPANCY - FACT: … | SOURCE A: … | SOURCE B: … | RESOLUTION: … | CONFIDENCE: … | ACTION NEEDED: …
OPPOSING CLAIM: … | SUPPORTING: … | CONTRADICTING: … | RESOLUTION: … | CONFIDENCE: …
UNRESOLVED: … | IMPACT: Minor/Moderate/Major | MITIGATION in lyrics: …
```

**Confidence levels:**

| Level | Definition | In lyrics |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| **High** | 3+ primary sources, all aligned | State as fact |
| **Medium** | 3+ sources, mix of primary and secondary | Hedge ("reportedly", "prosecutors said") |
| **Low** | Secondary only, or sources conflict | Flag for review |
| **Unverified** | Single source or no primary | Don't use without an explicit caveat |

### Where to look, by domain

Search across whichever of these the story touches. Each list is in rough tier order.

- **Legal:** indictments (the overt acts give a dated timeline; the counts give the charges), plea agreements (the statement of facts holds the defendant's own admissions), sentencing memos from both sides (aggravating and mitigating details, human background), verdicts, appeals, civil complaints, DPAs and NPAs. Sources: PACER ($0.10/page, capped at $3/document), CourtListener/RECAP, PlainSite, Justia, DOJ case pages, state court portals.
- **Financial:** SEC EDGAR filings. The 10-K's Risk Factors, Legal Proceedings, MD&A, and notes are where problems surface. 8-Ks cover material events (executive departures, cybersecurity incidents under Item 1.05, bankruptcy). Also DEF 14A (pay), Form 4 (insider trades), S-1, earnings-call transcripts, bankruptcy dockets, and historical stock prices around events. Financial press (WSJ, FT, Bloomberg, Reuters) is Tier 2.
- **Government:** DOJ and US Attorney press releases (charges, pleas, sentences), SEC litigation releases, FBI releases and wanted notices, CISA advisories, Treasury/OFAC sanctions, FTC and state AG actions, congressional testimony, and Inspector General and GAO reports. Extract the action, the defendant, the charges or sentence, and official quotes, then chase the underlying documents.
- **Historical:** contemporaneous documents and official investigations, newspapers from the time (Library of Congress Chronicling America, Google News Archive, newspapers.com, ProQuest, NYT archive), the National Archives, FBI Vault, CIA Reading Room, Archive.org, JSTOR and Google Scholar, and oral histories (StoryCorps, LoC, university projects). Contemporaneous coverage was written before anyone knew the outcome, so it captures the uncertainty of the moment.
- **Biographical:** the subject's own words first (interviews, memoirs, talks, blogs), then close sources (profiles by journalists who met them, interviews with colleagues and family, authorized biographies), then news and magazine profiles, then reference (LinkedIn for the career timeline, Crunchbase, public records, used carefully). For each subject, try to answer: origin, formation, motivation, method, relationships, turning points, contradictions, humanity.
- **Journalism:** investigative outlets (ProPublica, Reuters Investigates, ICIJ, Bellingcat, The Intercept), the major papers, and wire services (AP, Reuters, AFP). A strong source has a named author, multiple cited sources, referenced documents, a reputable outlet, a subject given the chance to respond, and fact kept separate from opinion. Red flags: aggregation without attribution, headlines that don't match the body, a single anonymous source, a retraction or correction, stale information.
- **Tech & security:** official project docs and announcements, mailing-list archives (the author's own words), git history (for dates), release notes, conference talks, LWN, and Ars Technica. For security: vendor advisories, CVE/NVD entries, victim incident reports, government attribution (CISA, FBI, NSA), research from security firms (Mandiant, CrowdStrike, Kaspersky GReAT, Talos, Microsoft), and DEF CON and Black Hat talks. Treat forums and leaked chats as Tier 4 that must be authenticated.
- **Primary-source voice** (any domain): official social accounts, blogs, forum and mailing-list posts with a consistent identity, and commit messages. Recover deleted material through the Wayback Machine or archive.today. Verify leaks through journalism that covered them, and record provenance.

### Free document sources

US federal court documents carry no copyright (17 U.S.C. § 105). PACER charges for convenience, not ownership. Exhaust these free sources before paying:

1. **DocumentCloud**: journalists upload purchased filings. Best for recent cases.
2. **CourtListener / RECAP**: a crowdsourced PACER archive with millions of documents.
3. **Scribd**: search "[case] indictment" or "[party] complaint".
4. **Justia**: appellate opinions and some district documents.
5. **Court websites**: "cases of interest" pages for high-profile cases.
6. **Agency sites**: DOJ press releases often link the indictment PDF. The SEC litigation pages hold complaints and settlements.
7. **Legal publishers**: Law360 trial coverage, CCH, Bloomberg Law's free filings.
8. **News archives**: investigations often embed or link their source documents.
9. **Academic**: SSRN appendices, law-school repositories, Archive.org.
10. **Specialty**: PlainSite, Docket Alarm (limited).

Search order: DocumentCloud → CourtListener → Scribd → court and agency sites → news archives.

### Citation and documentation

Keep two plain documents per album: **research notes** (facts by track) and a **source list** (full citations).

Each research-notes entry records: the fact statement, its primary citation, the verification matrix, page numbers for key quotes, context, a confidence level, any discrepancies, and open questions.

Citation formats:
```
Court:      [Case Name], [Court], [Document Type], Filed [Date], pp.[X-Y]. Retrieved [date]. Archive: [URL]
Historical: [Publication] - [Topic], [Date range], [Description]. Retrieved [date]. Archive: [URL]
```

Archive every URL (Wayback Machine or archive.today), because URLs die. Watch for date traps: time zones, fiscal versus calendar years, and announced versus occurred dates.

### Verification before writing

**Self-check (QC pass)** before handing off to the user:
- [ ] Every source URL opens and is archived
- [ ] Every direct quote is verbatim and matches the cited page
- [ ] Dates, numbers, and names agree across all sources and notes
- [ ] Every claim traces to a recorded source
- [ ] Primary sources used wherever they exist
- [ ] Each source and quote checked individually, not as a rolled-up "looks fine"

Report the results as High, Medium, Low, or Unverified, plus discrepancies and methodology gaps. Any critical failure blocks the handoff.

**Human verification gate:** the user must personally confirm the sources for each track before lyrics are generated. Present each track's sources as `[Name](URL) - what it supports` and ask the user to open each one, confirm the content, and confirm that nothing is fabricated. Never mark a source verified yourself. If the user finds a problem, keep the track pending: find a new URL, revise the lyric to match the source, or drop a fabricated source and rework the track. Date-stamp each verification.

Division of labor: the QC pass confirms that claims are *sourced* (links, verbatim quotes, consistent dates). The human judges whether the source is *right*, whether the interpretation is fair, and whether the tone is appropriate.

### How research feeds the lyrics

**Hard rules**
- **Narrator voice only.** Never write as a real person. Tell their story; don't speak as them.
- **No fabricated quotes.** Only verified statements appear as quotes.
- **Every claim traceable.** Each factual line maps to an entry in the research notes. Names, dates, and amounts must be exact.
- **Stay within the sources.** Don't let the lyrics claim more than the sources say. Hedge Medium-confidence facts.
- **Fair use:** short quotes for commentary, never wholesale reproduction.

**Three attribution modes**
```
Direct quote (verified):    He told the Times, "I never saw a dime"
Paraphrase (reported):      He claimed he didn't know, played ignorant
Narrator summary (multi):   The evidence mounted, day by day
```
Capture a real person's voice through the narrator: "I don't care about money, I just want the code to be free" becomes "He said he didn't care about the money / Just wanted the code to run free."

**Mine the research for:**
- **Specific human details:** not "he was smart" but "dropped out after one semester". Contradictions between public image and private reality. Habits, quirks, the decision that changed everything.
- **Admissions, regret, defiance, denial, blame** from interviews and plea statements.
- **Numbers and dates** that tell the story (losses, sentences, counts, years).
- **Period language** for historical tracks. Early 1900s formal ("a most unfortunate occurrence"), 1920s-30s slang ("on the level"), 1950s Cold War ("subversive elements"), 1980s excess ("greed is good"), 1990s tech optimism ("information superhighway"). Check headlines and speeches from the era.
- **Jargon that sings.** Source-domain terms make strong lines:
  - Legal: superseding indictment, overt acts, allocution, forfeiture, restitution, cooperation agreement, guidelines range
  - Financial: going concern, material adverse effect, restatement, impairment, clawback, golden parachute
  - Government: "unsealed today", "ill-gotten gains", "brought to justice", "acting in concert"
  - Journalism: "documents show", "declined to comment", "sources say"
  - Tech and security: fork, upstream, patch, root, zero-day, backdoor, payload, C2, exfil, lateral movement, persistence

**Handle with care**
- **Sensitive subjects:** don't glorify violence. Consider victims and their families and descendants. Use era-appropriate terms while noting how language has changed. Documenting something is not endorsing it.
- **Pronunciation:** names and places are where true-story tracks break. Spell every proper noun phonetically where needed (see references/suno/pronunciation.md).
- **Later edits:** rewriting, reordering, or deleting sections after generation can drop a sourced fact or scramble the chronology. Re-check against the research notes (see references/album/personas-and-covers.md).

**Release checklist additions:** all sources verified by the user; research notes and source list complete; every lyric checked against the sources; narrator voice throughout, with no impersonation. For writing craft and review, see references/lyrics/writing.md and references/lyrics/review.md.
